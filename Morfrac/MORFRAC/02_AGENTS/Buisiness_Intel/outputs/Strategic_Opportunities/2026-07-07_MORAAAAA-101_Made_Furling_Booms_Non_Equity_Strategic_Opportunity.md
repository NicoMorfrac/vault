# MORAAAAA-101 Made Furling Booms: Non-Equity Strategic Opportunity

Generated: 2026-07-07

## Strategic Objective

Evaluate whether MORFRAC should support a client's possible acquisition of Made Engineering / Made Systems furling boom products through a non-equity strategic role.

The objective is not to validate the acquisition for the client. The objective is to decide whether MORFRAC can create value through paid technical due diligence, bounded engineering support, DFM improvement, critical component supply, documentation/process improvement, spare parts, and retrofit/service support without becoming an unpaid engineering department or absorbing uncontrolled legacy liability.

## Executive Decision

Decision: HOLD, with a paid Phase 1 due diligence offer.

Confidence: Medium on strategic fit; low-to-medium on commercial viability until private data is reviewed.

Reason: Public evidence confirms an active product surface, visible product segmentation, project examples, dealer/service infrastructure, manuals/downloads, spare parts pages, and a clear fit with MORFRAC's sailing-hardware and retrofit-integration capabilities. Public evidence does not confirm sales volume, margins, warranty history, CAD/BOM quality, actual liability exposure, acquisition economics, or whether the client will accept paid scope boundaries.

Required next step: Send a paid due diligence information request and do not quote engineering, supply, exclusivity, royalty, or partner terms until MORFRAC has reviewed technical, commercial, legal/IP, and operational materials.

Main risk: MORFRAC becomes treated as a low-margin supplier while being expected to solve design, installation, support, warranty, and product-continuity problems outside its contractual control.

Best commercial model: External strategic technical and component partner, starting with fixed-fee due diligence, then fixed-scope engineering/NRE, then margin-protected critical component or spare-parts supply with MOQs, liability caps, and no legacy-design warranty.

What MORFRAC should avoid: Equity, joint venture, broad exclusivity without annual minimums, unpaid engineering, responsibility for sailmaking/mast/full-boom certification, open-ended support, and liability for third-party installation or legacy design defects.

## Joint Venture Evaluation Addendum

Added: 2026-07-07 after board request to evaluate whether a joint venture proposal fits MORFRAC.

### Strategic Objective

Determine whether a formal joint venture could ever be a better structure than the non-equity external technical/component partner model recommended above.

### Short answer

A joint venture does not fit MORFRAC as the default structure for the Made furling boom opportunity.

It could fit only as a later-stage, tightly scoped, non-operating commercial vehicle after due diligence and only if the opportunity proves materially larger than a supplier/engineering relationship. The current evidence does not justify recommending a JV now.

### Why a JV is not the default fit

Evidence from the public review and internal findings shows this is a geometry-sensitive, installation-sensitive, support-sensitive product category. That makes a JV risky because it can pull MORFRAC into whole-product responsibility without full control of sales, installation, warranty, dealer behavior, sailmaker execution, mast/vang interfaces, and legacy design quality.

The report's current HOLD decision remains valid because the missing information is still material:

- annual sales volume and margins
- product liability and warranty history
- CAD/BOM and documentation quality
- IP ownership and right to modify/manufacture
- supplier/tooling ownership
- dealer/service obligations
- hidden customer support burden
- client's willingness to pay for scoped due diligence and engineering

Until those are proven, a JV would add governance and liability complexity before the commercial base is validated.

### When a JV might fit

A JV should be considered only if all of the following are true:

- Phase 1 due diligence validates real sales traction, clean IP, transferable assets, acceptable warranty history, and attractive margins.
- The client brings something MORFRAC cannot efficiently access through contract alone, such as distribution ownership, acquisition control, dealer network, sales pipeline, or capital for inventory/tooling.
- MORFRAC's contribution is strategically material and defensible, such as exclusive component IP, engineered upgrade packages, manufacturing system redesign, or a repeatable service/retrofit platform.
- The JV has a narrow commercial purpose, not broad responsibility for the whole furling boom product.
- The JV can isolate product liability, legacy warranty, installation liability, and working capital exposure away from MORFRAC's core business.
- MORFRAC has veto rights over technical changes, quality standards, supplier substitutions, and product claims that depend on MORFRAC engineering.
- The economics exceed what MORFRAC could earn through paid due diligence, NRE, component margin, retainer, and limited exclusivity.

### JV forms considered

| JV form | Fit for MORFRAC | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Full product company JV | Poor fit | Too much exposure to sales, warranty, installation, support, and legacy design without enough current evidence. |
| Manufacturing JV | Weak fit now | Could tie MORFRAC to volume, inventory, tooling, and supplier risk before demand/margins are validated. |
| Distribution/service JV | Weak fit | High operational burden and third-party installation/support liability risk. |
| Product improvement/IP JV | Possible later | Only if MORFRAC-developed improvements create measurable value and IP rights are clean. |
| Upgrade/spare-parts JV | Possible later | More bounded; could monetize recurring parts/service if demand and warranty boundaries are validated. |
| Non-operating commercial JV for a defined upgrade package | Best possible JV shape | Narrow purpose, measurable economics, lower operating burden, and easier liability boundaries than a full product JV. |

### Best approach if the client pushes for a JV

MORFRAC should not reject the conversation outright, but should sequence it behind paid validation:

1. State that MORFRAC is not ready to discuss a JV before due diligence.
2. Offer Phase 1 paid due diligence as the first gate.
3. If due diligence is positive, offer Phase 2 engineering/component pilot under ordinary commercial contracts.
4. Only after a successful pilot, evaluate whether a JV produces better economics than:
   - NRE fees
   - component margin
   - spare-parts margin
   - paid technical retainer
   - limited exclusivity with annual minimums
   - royalty/success fee on MORFRAC-developed improvements
5. If a JV is still justified, limit it to a defined package such as a MORFRAC-engineered Made upgrade/spare-parts platform, not the whole product business.

### Preferred JV structure if justified later

If a JV becomes commercially justified, the best structure would be:

- Scope: defined upgrade, critical component, or spare-parts/service package for Made furling booms.
- Purpose: monetize MORFRAC-developed improvements and controlled component supply, not operate the full boom company.
- MORFRAC contribution: engineering package, component design, supplier qualification, documentation/process package, and selected production.
- Client contribution: product ownership, acquisition rights, sales channel, customer/dealer access, working capital, product liability insurance, and commercial operations.
- Governance: MORFRAC technical veto on any component, process, claim, or installation instruction relying on MORFRAC engineering.
- Economics: MORFRAC receives paid NRE before IP transfer, component margin, and either royalty/profit share on the defined package.
- Liability: JV or client carries product liability and legacy warranty; MORFRAC liability capped to defective MORFRAC deliverables.
- Exclusivity: limited to the defined package and conditional on annual minimum revenue/purchase commitments.
- Exit: termination rights if volume, payment, quality, insurance, or liability conditions fail.

### Revised recommendation

The best approach is not a JV proposal now. The best approach is a phased strategic partner proposal with an explicit JV option gate:

- Phase 1: paid due diligence.
- Phase 2: fixed-scope engineering / documentation / DFM package.
- Phase 3: component or spare-parts pilot.

This keeps MORFRAC commercially firm while leaving room for upside if the acquisition target proves stronger than current public evidence can validate.

## Commercial Relevance

### Why the opportunity matters

Furling booms sit at the intersection of high-value hardware, engineering integration, sailmaker requirements, rigging/yard installation, service/spares, and owner training. That makes the category commercially more attractive than commodity deck hardware, but also more dangerous. The margin is in technical risk reduction, component quality, repeatable documentation, critical parts, and support systems. The risk is inheriting whole-product responsibility without whole-product control.

### Why competitors or incumbents may fail

Confirmed weakness should not be overstated. Public competitor pages show that serious players understand service, manuals, installation, and geometry. However, both public market commentary and internal findings indicate the category is still sensitive to setup, handling, support continuity, and interface quality. Weakness is therefore not necessarily "competitors are bad." The weakness is that the category structurally depends on multi-party execution, and that execution can fail at interfaces:

- boom manufacturer to sailmaker
- boom manufacturer to dealer/installer
- dealer/installer to mast/vang geometry
- owner procedure to system limits
- spare-parts support to legacy product continuity
- acquisition owner to engineering/manufacturing continuity

### Why MORFRAC may have an advantage

MORFRAC has plausible advantage where the problem is engineering-intensive and bounded:

- load-path and mechanical-system review
- CNC and small-batch critical component development
- aluminum/titanium/stainless/polymer hardware competence
- supplier qualification and traceability
- documentation and installation-process improvement
- B2B yard/rigging/refit support
- retrofit engineering
- product simplification and reliability improvement

MORFRAC does not automatically have advantage in full-boom certification, sailmaking, mast design, dealer management, global warranty administration, or customer-support operations. Those must remain excluded unless explicitly contracted and controlled.

1. Start with paid due diligence, not free advice.
2. Quote engineering and supply separately.
