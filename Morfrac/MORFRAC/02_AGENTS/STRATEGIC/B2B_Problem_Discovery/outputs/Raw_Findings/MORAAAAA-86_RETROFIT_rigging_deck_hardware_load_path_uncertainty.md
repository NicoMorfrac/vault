# SUMMARY

Retrofit work around standing-rigging attachments and adjacent deck hardware repeatedly runs into the same combined failure mode: uncertain internal structure, difficult access, hidden moisture/corrosion risk, and no validated load path for the new hardware geometry. The symptom looks like "rebedding" or "adding a backing plate," but the root problem is that installers and owners often do not know what substrate, reinforcement, or load-transfer method is actually inside the deck or hull before modifying the rig or hardware.

---

# EVIDENCE

## Directly observed evidence

- In the YBW `leaky chainplates` thread, the owner reports chainplates "glassed in underside of the deck," says rebedding requires cutting away fiberglass, and asks whether sealing from the edges is enough. Multiple replies warn that leaks around these fittings lead to crevice corrosion, hidden failures, and mast-loss risk, with one respondent describing a near-mast-loss after leaks were ignored.
- In the YBW `Cracks around chainplates moulded into gunwhales` thread, the owner reports inaccessible molded-in chainplates, a leak into the cabin, gelcoat cracking at the deck emergence point, and concern about hidden rust/corrosion. Replies explicitly advise determining whether the cracks indicate upward deck distortion or structural movement before treating the leak as a simple sealing job.
- In the YBW `Fitting different winches to cabin roof` thread, the retrofit problem is not the winch itself but inaccessible structure: the owner wants to change winch size/pattern but states there is "no access from underneath without surgery" and does not know whether a buried metal plate exists or how the original winch load is being carried.
- In the YBW `Winch backing plates` thread, the owner reports molded winch pads with "no reinforcement below the deck moulding." Replies discuss uncertainty over whether the core is ply or balsa, whether the deck is solid laminate, how to overdrill/fill/redrill to prevent water ingress, and what backing material is adequate for sheet-winch loads.
- In the Boat Design Net `Chain plates fastened to hulls or deck fittings` discussion, contributors describe through-deck chainplates as difficult to keep from leaking, with water entering the core and damaging the deck, and frame external/composite alternatives partly as maintainability solutions.
- In the Boat Design Net `Chainplates and how to attach them to the hull` discussion, a naval architect reduces the retrofit question to load transfer and shear path justification, while another experienced builder notes repeated repairs of leaky deck penetrations and warns that glass-encased chainplates are hard to inspect and prone to oxygen-starved corrosion.

## Repeated pattern

- The recurrence is not just "leaks happen." The repeated pattern is that retrofits expose missing information about core material, buried backing structure, load direction, and inspection access.
- Installation decisions repeatedly depend on destructive exploration: cutting liners, drilling pilot holes, removing cabinetry, opening laminate, or dropping the rig to inspect concealed metal and wet core.
- Owners and builders repeatedly treat deck-hardware upgrades, winch swaps, chainplate resealing, and chainplate relocation as linked structural problems rather than isolated hardware replacements.

# ROOT CAUSE ANALYSIS

## Symptom

- Leaking chainplates
- Cracked laminate around deck penetrations
- Flexing deck hardware zones
- Unclear backing-plate requirements
- Difficulty upgrading winches or changing rig attachment geometry

## Likely root operational causes

- Buried or inaccessible reinforcement makes verification of existing load paths difficult.
- Through-deck penetrations combine structural and sealing duties in one detail, so water ingress and load-transfer problems accumulate together.
- Older boats often lack documentation on core type, embedded plates, laminate schedule, or original reinforcement geometry.
- Retrofit geometry changes alter bolt patterns, pull angles, and local bending without a validated engineering check.
- Encased stainless components are difficult to inspect, allowing concealed crevice corrosion to persist until disassembly.

---

# OPERATIONAL IMPACT

- Retrofit jobs become exploratory structural work rather than straightforward hardware replacement.
- Rigging changes can require mast removal, liner cutting, cabinetry removal, or laminate opening before the installer can confirm the attachment method.
- Yards and riggers inherit failure risk when they cannot verify hidden structure but are still expected to re-use or modify it.
- Water ingress can turn a sealing issue into deck-core damage, bulkhead degradation, or concealed chainplate corrosion, increasing rework scope and downtime.
- Lack of validated load paths increases the chance of overbuilding, underbuilding, or delaying the job while owners seek ad hoc advice.
