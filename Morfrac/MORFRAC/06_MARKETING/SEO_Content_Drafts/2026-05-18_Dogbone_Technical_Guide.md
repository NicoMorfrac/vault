# Dogbone Technical Guide

Dogbones are compact hard-point connectors used to join textile elements without forcing the connection through the geometry of a conventional metal shackle. In high-load sailing systems, that matters because the connector is not just a fastening detail. It influences rope bend radius, local wear, assembly speed, and how cleanly a soft connection integrates with the rest of the rigging package.

For teams evaluating a [dogbone product family](https://www.morfrac.com/dogbone), the useful question is not simply "which size should I buy?" The better question is how the connector geometry, material, and textile pairing affect the reliability of the complete connection.

## What a dogbone does in a sailing system

A dogbone creates a defined hard interface inside a soft-connection system. Instead of relying on a traditional shackle pin and bow, the assembly uses a shaped component that works with loops, strops, or other textile elements to create a secure connection with less unnecessary hardware mass and less geometric mismatch.

In practice, dogbones are used where the designer wants:

- a compact connection package
- clean integration with Dyneema or other textile rigging elements
- reduced clutter compared with larger metal fittings
- a controlled interface between soft and hard components

This makes them relevant in performance-oriented systems where weight, space, and rigging efficiency all matter, but where the connection still needs to be mechanically predictable.

## When a dogbone is a better choice than a conventional shackle

A dogbone is generally worth considering when the rest of the system is already moving toward textile architecture. If the load path is carried through soft loops, lashings, or spliced elements, a dogbone can make the connection more compact and more coherent than inserting a standard shackle only because it is familiar.

That does not mean dogbones replace every metal connector. Conventional shackles still make sense when fast pin access, broad compatibility, or established deck-hardware interfaces are the main requirement. The decision is application-driven:

- use a dogbone when the priority is a streamlined textile connection
- use a conventional shackle when the surrounding hardware is designed around pin-based metal interfaces
- review the entire load path before changing one connector in isolation

The right comparison is not "dogbone versus shackle" in the abstract. It is whether the chosen connector supports the geometry and service conditions of the complete assembly.

## Why geometry matters more than marketing language

In dogbone applications, geometry is not cosmetic. The form of the connector affects how the textile element seats, how sharply fibers are forced to turn, and whether the assembly remains stable under repeated cycling.

Poor geometry can create avoidable stress concentration, uneven bearing, or abrasion at the interface. A better geometry supports:

- stable seating of the textile element
- a more controlled rope path
- reduced tendency toward premature local wear
- easier repeatable rigging

That is why selection should begin with the connection layout, not with headline claims about strength. If the geometry does not suit the splice, loop, or surrounding hardware, a stronger material alone does not solve the problem.

## Material selection: aluminum, titanium, steel, and the application tradeoff

Material choice should follow the operating environment and the performance target of the assembly. MORFRAC's current dogbone positioning includes hard-anodized aluminum and titanium on the main landing page, with custom options also referencing 6082-T6, 7075-T6, AISI316L stainless steel, and Ti-6Al-4V titanium on the custom section of the range.

From a selection standpoint, the tradeoff is straightforward:

- aluminum is typically attractive when low weight and efficient corrosion-resistant finishing are priorities
- titanium is attractive when buyers are optimizing for high-end corrosion resistance, low mass, and premium-performance hardware packages
- stainless steel may still be relevant in some custom cases where broader metal-hardware compatibility or a specific design brief matters

The mistake is to treat material as a branding decision. Material must be matched to use environment, contact surfaces, expected inspection interval, and the rest of the hardware package around it.

## Dyneema compatibility and soft-connection thinking

Dogbones make the most sense when they are treated as one part of a textile connection system rather than as a standalone fitting. Dyneema is frequently considered in these systems because it enables light, strong, low-stretch soft connections, but the connector still has to respect how the textile element behaves in service.

When pairing a dogbone with Dyneema or another textile element, review:

- the finished diameter of the loop or strop, not just nominal rope size
- splice and bury geometry
- how the textile bears on the connector under working load
- how easy the assembly is to inspect after installation
- whether repeated movement will create localized wear points

This is where engineering discipline matters. A visually compact assembly is not automatically a good assembly. The connection has to be easy to seat correctly, hard to rig incorrectly, and realistic to inspect over time.

## Load and sizing considerations without guesswork

Load selection should never be reduced to "bigger is safer." Oversizing can create a bulky or awkward connection that does not seat well with the textile component, while undersizing creates obvious risk. The correct approach is to size the dogbone inside the context of the full system:

- define the real load case, including dynamic behavior if relevant
- confirm the textile element and connector are dimensionally compatible
- check that surrounding hardware does not introduce sharper bends or edge contact
- review clearance, articulation, and access for installation
- keep the selected connector consistent with the rest of the rigging specification

Because published product content and custom options can differ by application, the best route is to treat sizing as an engineering matching exercise rather than a catalog shortcut. ## Custom dogbones and when standard catalog logic is not enough

Some applications do not fit neatly into an off-the-shelf hardware decision. That is especially true when the connector has to match an existing rigging geometry, a specific textile package, or a constrained installation envelope.

In those cases, a custom dogbone route can be more sensible than forcing a standard part into a compromised setup. Customization is useful when the project requires:

- a specific shape or dimensional envelope
- a defined material choice
- integration with a tailored textile element
- alignment with a larger engineered rigging package

For specifiers and refit teams, this is often the difference between "a part that fits" and "a connection that works properly."

## How to evaluate a dogbone supplier

Technical buyers should not evaluate dogbones only by appearance or price. The stronger signal is whether the supplier understands the interaction between connector geometry, textile behavior, and the actual use case.

Useful evaluation questions include:

- Is the product family clearly positioned for textile connection use?
- Are material options explicit?
- Is there evidence of custom-engineering capability when the standard range is not enough?
- Does the supplier talk about geometry, wear, and system fit instead of only generic strength language?

Those questions help separate commodity hardware listings from engineered connection products.
